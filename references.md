# 1. Sanskrit Text-to-Speech (TTS) and Speech Synthesis

- **Early Sanskrit TTS (Samvachak)** – *Mishra et al. (2011)* developed the first Sanskrit TTS system “Samvachak” using the Festival engine ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=Abstract%3A%20In%20this%20paper%20the,the%20Festival%20Speech%20Synthesis%20Engine)). They noted unique challenges, including Sanskrit’s complex sandhi (euphonic word joining) and rich morphology, which require extensive text normalization and phonological rules ([[2106.05852] Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2106.05852#:~:text=,investigate%20the%20role%20of%20different)) ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=Studies%2C%20JNU,the%20Festival%20Speech%20Synthesis%20Engine)). This work highlighted the need for Sanskrit-specific linguistic preprocessing (e.g. G2P conversion, syllabification) since no prior TTS existed for Sanskrit ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=Sanskrit%20is%20a%20heritage%20language,9)).

- **Neural Sanskrit TTS (Low-Resource)** – *Debnath et al. (2022)* built an end-to-end Sanskrit TTS with Tacotron2 and WaveGlow, using transfer learning from English ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03558#:~:text=European%20languages%20like%20English%20and,Sanskrit%20in%20low%20resource%20settings)) ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=languages%20like%20English%20and%20Spanish,38%20from%2037)). Despite only ~2.5 hours of training data (recitations of the *Bhagavad Gita*), the system achieved a mean opinion score (MOS) of 3.38 from evaluators familiar with Sanskrit ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=training%20and%20inference%3B%203,5%20hours%20only)). This result is promising for a low-resource setting, and it underscores the effectiveness of fine-tuning pre-trained TTS models for Sanskrit speech ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=languages%20like%20English%20and%20Spanish,38%20from%2037)). The authors affirm that prior to their work, *Mishra et al.*’s system was the only notable Sanskrit TTS effort ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=Sanskrit%20is%20a%20heritage%20language,9)).

- **Prosody/Expressive TTS for Sanskrit** – There have been attempts to synthesize more *expressive* Sanskrit speech, especially for poetry and chants. *Rama & Lakshmanan (2010)* proposed an algorithm for *“text-to-tuneful-speech”* synthesis of Sanskrit verses ([[1409.4169] An Algorithm Based on Empirical Methods, for Text-to-Tuneful-Speech Synthesis of Sanskrit Verse](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/1409.4169#:~:text=,contents%20of%20Sanskrit%20verses%20otherwise)). Their system uses a small prerecorded audio unit database and empirical rules to produce *chanting* with correct tune and rhythm ([[1409.4169] An Algorithm Based on Empirical Methods, for Text-to-Tuneful-Speech Synthesis of Sanskrit Verse](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/1409.4169#:~:text=language%20itself,contents%20of%20Sanskrit%20verses%20otherwise)). This approach specifically targets Sanskrit poetic recitation, rendering verses with a melodic intonation – useful for Vedic chants or shlokas – and was motivated by making Sanskrit literature (in verse form) accessible to the visually impaired ([[1409.4169] An Algorithm Based on Empirical Methods, for Text-to-Tuneful-Speech Synthesis of Sanskrit Verse](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/1409.4169#:~:text=language%20itself,contents%20of%20Sanskrit%20verses%20otherwise)). Another example in a related classical language is a unit-selection TTS for *Pali* (Mache et al. 2016), which built a speech corpus and concatenated phoneme/syllable units; this achieved high naturalness by minimizing prosody mismatches across unit boundaries ([](https://www.iosrjournals.org/iosr-jce/papers/Vol18-issue3/Version-1/G1803013542.pdf#:~:text=Abstract%20%3A%20We%20introduced%20a,An)) ([](https://www.iosrjournals.org/iosr-jce/papers/Vol18-issue3/Version-1/G1803013542.pdf#:~:text=important%20advantage%20of%20this%20approach,this%20system%20is%20the%20improved)).

- **Phonetic Challenges** – Research highlights that Sanskrit’s phonetics pose challenges for TTS. The language undergoes **sandhi** (phonetic assimilation at word boundaries) and has free word order, which complicate text-to-phoneme mapping and phrasing ([[2106.05852] Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2106.05852#:~:text=,investigate%20the%20role%20of%20different)). Sanskrit orthography is highly phonetic but still requires handling of visarga, anusvara, and vowel length distinctions. Mishra et al. developed dedicated front-end modules – e.g. a rule-based *grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) converter* and a text normalizer – to handle Sanskrit’s writing system and convert numbers, abbreviations, etc. into pronounceable words ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=knowledge%20discourse%20of%20Sanskrit,phoneme%20word%20by%20word%2C%20thus)) ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=Abstract%3A%20Though%20Sanskrit%20writing%20system,Normalizer%20does%20some)). They also implemented *syllabification and basic stress assignment* in the phonetic output for their Festival-based system ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=Abstract%3A%20The%20authors%20of%20this,Festival%20framework%2C%20which%20is)). (Notably, this “stress” was a simple heuristic and not the classical Vedic pitch accent.) These linguistic pre-processing steps are crucial for accurate Sanskrit TTS, ensuring that long vs. short vowels, visargas, and consonant clusters are handled correctly in synthesis.

# 2. Prosody Modeling in Speech Synthesis (Metrical Structure & Pitch)

- **Poetic Intonation Patterns** – Speech synthesis for poetry requires modeling intonation and rhythm that differ markedly from prose. Koch et al. (2022) note that *“poetic speech”* uses shorter intonation units, more frequent pauses, and repeated pitch patterns – a phenomenon dubbed *“poetic intonation”* ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/koch22_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=regulated%20form%20with%20parallel%20structures,syn%02thesis%20model%20is%20developed%20within)). For example, in metered verse, syllables often follow a regular rhythmic length (e.g. **laghu** and **guru** in Sanskrit meter) and exhibit recurring tunes. A TTS trained only on prose may struggle with these patterns ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/koch22_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=that%20poetic%20speech%20differs%20from,syn%02thesis%20model%20is%20developed%20within)). To address this, **PoeticTTS** (Koch et al.) clones the prosody from a human recitation of a poem and then allows fine-grained prosody manipulation in a human-in-the-loop setup ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/koch22_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=by%20cloning%20prosodic%20values%20from,well%20as%20in%20human%20studies)). By fine-tuning a neural TTS model on poetry data, they capture genre-specific intonation, enabling nearly human-like rendition of literary verse with controlled variations in rhythm and pitch ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/koch22_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=synthetic%20speech%20in%20a%20human,well%20as%20in%20human%20studies)) ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/koch22_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=regulated%20form%20with%20parallel%20structures,syn%02thesis%20model%20is%20developed%20within)). This demonstrates the importance of prosody-aware training for structured texts like poetry.

- **Intonation Templates for TTS** – Another approach is to build explicit models of pitch contours. Lee et al. (2022) propose *Into-TTS*, which introduces **predefined intonation templates** for an end-to-end TTS system ([[2204.01271] Into-TTS : Intonation Template Based Prosody Control System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01271#:~:text=,the%20text%20encoder%20output%2C%20synthesizes)). In their method, the training speech data are automatically clustered by intonation pattern (unsupervised), yielding a set of representative pitch/rhythm templates ([[2204.01271] Into-TTS : Intonation Template Based Prosody Control System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01271#:~:text=intonations,Main%20contributions%20of%20our)). They add an *intonation predictor* (to select an appropriate template for a given input text) and an *intonation encoder* (to enforce that contour in synthesis) into the Tacotron2 framework ([[2204.01271] Into-TTS : Intonation Template Based Prosody Control System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01271#:~:text=templates,c%29%20incorporating%20a%20pre)). This architecture lets one generate the same sentence with different intonations by selecting different templates. The result is an intuitive prosody control that improves subjective naturalness and gives users influence over the speech’s melody ([[2204.01271] Into-TTS : Intonation Template Based Prosody Control System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01271#:~:text=,the%20text%20encoder%20output%2C%20synthesizes)) ([[2204.01271] Into-TTS : Intonation Template Based Prosody Control System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01271#:~:text=speech%20abiding%20the%20requested%20intonation,Audio%20samples%20are%20available%20at)). Such template-based prosody control could be adapted to Sanskrit verses by designing templates corresponding to common *chandas* patterns or chanting tunes.

- **Unsupervised Prosody Tags** – Recent research also explores learning prosody representations directly from data. Korotkova et al. (2024) introduce an *unsupervised word-level prosodic markup* method: a model learns to cluster acoustic prosodic features and assign discrete tags to words, capturing fine-grained intonation events ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/korotkova24_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=follows%3A%20%E2%80%A2%20We%20introduce%20an,examples%20on%20our%20demo%20page2)). These learned prosody tags, when fed into a TTS system, make the synthesized speech more expressive and allow explicit control over pitch patterns ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/korotkova24_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20We%20introduce%20an%20unsupervised,the%20examples%20on%20our%20demo)). In their work, integrating the prosody markup into the TTS training led to richer intonation in outputs (compared to baseline models without prosody input) ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/korotkova24_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=prosody,the%20examples%20on%20our%20demo)). This line of work shows how *computational prosody models* (whether via tags, embeddings, or auxiliary inputs) can enforce rhythmic and pitch constraints. For a Sanskrit TTS, one could imagine tags indicating metrical positions (e.g. a tag for line-break cadence or a specific *vṛtta* meter pattern) to guide the TTS in modulating duration and pitch per the meter.

- **Modeling **Swara**** (Pitch Accents)** – Sanskrit Vedic chanting includes marked pitch accents (*udātta*, *anudātta*, *svarita*) on syllables ([Vedic accent - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_accent#:~:text=Vedic%20accent%20,Sanskrit%20grammarians%20into%20three%20qualities)), essentially embedding a musical *swara* pattern into the speech. Prosody modeling for such chanting overlaps with **singing synthesis**, since the pitch must follow a prescribed melodic curve. In singing voice TTS, it’s known that the range of fundamental frequency (F0) and phoneme duration is much wider than in normal speech ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/okamoto24_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=Abstract%20Singing%20voice%20synthesis%20,based)), and models must be able to handle sustained vowels and large pitch jumps. Similarly, a Sanskrit chanting TTS must accommodate extended vowel lengths (for *guru* syllables or prolonged chants) and specific pitch movements on accented syllables. Approaches from **singing synthesis** (e.g. FastSpeech-based models that take musical note inputs ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/okamoto24_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=only%20a%20TTS%20corpus%2C%20some,JSUT%20cor%02pus%20confirm%20that%20the))) or explicit pitch control as above can be applied. Although dedicated research on *Vedic chant TTS* is sparse, incorporating the known tonal rules from Vedic recitation manuals into a TTS system (e.g. raising pitch on *udātta* syllables and falling on *svarita*) is a plausible strategy. The integration of measured melodic contours (as “pitch tracks”) with TTS has precedent in experiments on “speech-to-song” adaptation, and could be employed to ensure synthesized Sanskrit chants respect traditional *svara* patterns.

# 3. Computational Approaches to Sanskrit Metrics and Poetic Structures

- **Meter Identification Algorithms** – A number of works tackle the automatic detection of Sanskrit **Chandas** (metrical patterns) from text, which is valuable for any prosody-aware system. For example, *Terdalkar & Bhattacharya (2022)* introduced **Chandojñānam**, a web-based meter identification system ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=1%20Introduction%20Majority%20of%20the,the%20correctness%20to%20some%20extent)). Given a Sanskrit verse (input as text or even via OCR from an image), it determines the meter by analyzing the sequence of light (laghu) and heavy (guru) syllables. The tool outputs the metrical pattern (often in a mnemonic like “LGG LGL ...” form) and even provides a **scansion** – a visual marking of the long/short syllable pattern ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=Abstract%20We%20present%20Chandoj%C3%B1%C4%81nam%2C%20a,It%20is%20also)) ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=1%20Introduction%20Majority%20of%20the,the%20correctness%20to%20some%20extent)). Chandojñānam’s backend uses a combination of dictionary lookup and pattern-matching algorithms with tolerance for errors ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=The%20algorithm%20used%20by%20our,These%20are%20explained%20in%20the)). If an input line doesn’t exactly fit a known meter, it can find the closest metrical match using fuzzy string matching, which can help flag possible text errors ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=We%20argue%20that%20a%20non,1%20Motivation)). This is not only useful for analyzing poetry, but also for “enforcing” metrical correctness: e.g., in a TTS scenario, the identified meter could inform the duration model to ensure the pattern of short/long timing is respected in synthesis.

- **Prior Work on Chandas Detection** – The development of Chandas identification has a lineage of tools from different researchers. Chandojñānam’s authors survey that earlier attempts (e.g. *Mishra 2007*; *Melnad et al. 2013*) were web interfaces for meter recognition that have since become defunct ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=There%20have%20been%20several%20efforts,2020%29%20and%20Neill)). More recent ones by *Rajagopalan (2020)* and *Neill (2022)* offered both web and library versions ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=Some%20of%20these%20tools%20,an%20input%2C%20limiting%20its%20usability)). Rajagopalan’s tool, for instance, provided a user-friendly interface for analyzing meter in Sanskrit verses ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=match%20at%20L986%20Shreevatsa%20Rajagopalan,com%2Fvirtualvinodh%2Faksharamukha)). However, these earlier systems often assumed one verse at a time input, limiting batch processing ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=More%20recent%20works%20,laghu%20and%20guru%20syllables%20respectively)), and did not handle noisy input. Chandojñānam built on these efforts by adding features like processing entire texts, OCR integration, and fuzzy matching for error tolerance. In an evaluation on a corpus of classical verses, Chandojñānam achieved higher accuracy in meter identification (98%+) than the prior tools (which scored ~80–92%) ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=match%20at%20L859%20the%20cases%2C,available%20with%20the%20source%20code)) ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=the%20cases%2C%20performing%20better%20than,available%20with%20the%20source%20code)). This shows active progress in **computational prosody** for Sanskrit – these algorithms make it feasible to automatically tag a large corpus of Sanskrit poetry with the correct meter, which could be leveraged in NLP pipelines or TTS front-ends.

- **Linguistic Studies on Sanskrit Meter** – The foundation for modeling Sanskrit’s rhythmic structure comes from prosody in traditional Sanskrit grammar and modern linguistics. An influential study by *Ashwini Deo (2007)* examined **the metrical organization of Classical Sanskrit verse** ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=Ashwini%20S%20Deo,Linux%20Journal)) ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=Matthias%20Lee,tool%20for%20metrical%20analysis%20of)). Deo highlighted three striking properties of Sanskrit’s classical meters: **(a)** they often consist of fixed sequences of syllable lengths without an obvious repeating sub-pattern (i.e. *aperiodicity*), **(b)** a given metrical template must be followed *exactly* by a verse with no substitutions (*invariance*), and **(c)** there is a *rich repertoire* of hundreds of distinct meters in the tradition ([The metrical organization of Classical Sanskrit verse | Journal of Linguistics | Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/metrical-organization-of-classical-sanskrit-verse/2EF882F88379615ADF5674923189098B#:~:text=In%20generative%20metrics%2C%20a%20meter,claim%20that%20in%20spite%20of)). In simpler terms, unlike English prosody which might have a small set of repeating feet (like iambic or trochaic), Sanskrit versification can use very specific patterns of laghu/guru syllables that don’t repeat in a cycle, and poets strictly adhere to those patterns. Despite this surface complexity, Deo argues that Sanskrit meters still conform to universal principles of **temporal organization** of speech ([The metrical organization of Classical Sanskrit verse | Journal of Linguistics | Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/metrical-organization-of-classical-sanskrit-verse/2EF882F88379615ADF5674923189098B#:~:text=between%20600%E2%80%93700%3A%20RICH%20REPERTOIRE,to%20existing%20assumptions%20about%20metrical)) – essentially, they can be analyzed with similar rhythmic models used for other languages, once the right representation is chosen. This kind of linguistic insight is useful for computational modeling: it suggests that a TTS system or prosody model can’t assume a simple periodic stress foot, but it can treat the entire line as a sequence to be timed according to the template. It also implies that any algorithm generating or validating Sanskrit metrical text (or speech) needs a database of hundreds of patterns (such as those encoded in Pingala’s *Chandaḥśāstra* or later compilations) – which is exactly what tools like Chandojñānam incorporate. There have also been algorithms to *generate* or manipulate text with respect to meter (e.g., an algorithm to convert Sanskrit prose to metrical verse ([[PDF] A Computational Algorithm based on Empirical Analysis, that ...](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Computational-Algorithm-based-on-Empirical-that-Rama-Lakshmanan/28c972038da21ff5d7d722bd46c94657eb99933a#:~:text=,metrical%20rules%20of%20Sanskrit%20prosody))), reflecting the interplay of syntax and prosody unique to Sanskrit’s poetic form. All these computational approaches treat **Chandas** as a first-class constraint – which is crucial if we want a TTS to read out a verse in a way that honors the rhythmic structure intended by the poet.

# 4. Datasets and Tools for Sanskrit TTS Development

**Open Speech Datasets for Sanskrit:**

- **IIT Bombay’s *Vāksañcayaḥ* Corpus (78 hours)** – A significant resource is the new **Sanskrit speech corpus** released by IIT Bombay (Adiga et al. 2021) ([Vāksañcayaḥ ~ Sanskrit Speech Corpus by IIT Bombay](https://www.cse.iitb.ac.in/~asr/#:~:text=V%C4%81ksa%C3%B1caya%E1%B8%A5%20,radio%20program%2C%20extempore%20discourse%2C%20etc)) ([[2106.05852] Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2106.05852#:~:text=automatic%20speech%20recognition%20,Finally)). It contains over *78 hours* of recorded Sanskrit audio (45,000+ sentences) at 22 kHz, covering a variety of content – readings from classical literature (across various Śāstras), modern stories, radio news, and extempore speech ([Vāksañcayaḥ ~ Sanskrit Speech Corpus by IIT Bombay](https://www.cse.iitb.ac.in/~asr/#:~:text=V%C4%81ksa%C3%B1caya%E1%B8%A5%20,radio%20program%2C%20extempore%20discourse%2C%20etc)). This corpus was originally created for ASR research, where it enabled the first large-scale Sanskrit speech recognition study ([[2106.05852] Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2106.05852#:~:text=automatic%20speech%20recognition%20,Finally)), but it is equally valuable for TTS. The audio is aligned with transcripts, and the dataset captures many linguistic phenomena of Sanskrit (sandhi, long compounds, etc.) in real pronunciation ([[2106.05852] Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2106.05852#:~:text=automatic%20speech%20recognition%20,Finally)). It’s available for research use under a Creative Commons license, making it a prime dataset for training and evaluating Sanskrit TTS models. Having a corpus with diverse speakers and topics also helps in building robust prosody models, since it isn’t limited to one style of recitation.

- **Bhagavad Gita Recitation (2.5 hours)** – A smaller but culturally important dataset is the **Bhagavad Gita audio corpus** used by Debnath et al. (2022) for their TTS ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=The%20dataset%20used%20is%20the,given%20in%20Table%20%2024)). This consists of a single male voice reading all 18 chapters of the Gita in Sanskrit, totalling about 2 hours 35 minutes of speech (segmented into ~1500 utterances) ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=The%20dataset%20used%20is%20the,given%20in%20Table%20%2024)). The audio clips (originally from an online source ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=arXiv%3A1912.02461%2C%202019.%20,246))) come with text alignments. While limited in duration, this dataset is rich in *intonation patterns of shloka recitation* – the Gita verses are chanted in a consistent meter (Anuṣṭubh) and style. Thus, it’s especially useful for research on *prosody in chanting*. In fact, Debnath et al. showed that even with this small dataset, a fine-tuned neural TTS can produce intelligible and natural Sanskrit speech ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=training%20and%20inference%3B%203,5%20hours%20only)). This Gita corpus could serve as a benchmarking set for evaluating how well a TTS handles rhythmic and melodic aspects of Sanskrit chanting.

- **Community-Contributed Corpora** – Beyond official academic releases, the Indic NLP community has compiled various speech datasets for Sanskrit. For example, a dataset of *Sanskrit audiobooks and movie dialogues (~60 hours)* has been used to train Wav2Vec2 models ([Sanskrit Audio Wav2Vec2 & Translation - Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/code/stpeteishii/sanskrit-audio-wav2vec2-translation#:~:text=Sanskrit%20Audio%20Wav2Vec2%20%26%20Translation,60%20hours%20of%20audio%20data)), and a *27-hour corpus of Sanskrit news broadcasts* (All India Radio news readings) has been made available on Kaggle ([Sanskrit Audio Dataset for Transcription - Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/datasets/comments/15v4t64/sanskrit_audio_dataset_for_transcription/#:~:text=Sharing%20the%20dataset%20containing%2027,information%20on%20the%20dataset%20page)). These datasets provide more **variety in style** – audiobooks may feature narrative prose, while news audio provides a more modern, possibly faster-paced Sanskrit with a different prosody than traditional chanting. Having multiple datasets allows researchers to test TTS systems across different domains (e.g., shloka recitation vs. news reading) to ensure versatility. We also note that similar speech resources are emerging for other classical languages of India: e.g., there are efforts to collect Prakrit/Pali recordings and even ancient Tamil Sangam literature recitations, though Sanskrit currently has the largest publicly available resources. 

- **Evaluation and Benchmarking Data** – Currently, there is no standardized *benchmark dataset* exclusively for “prosody-rich” Sanskrit TTS evaluation (akin to a Blizzard Challenge for Sanskrit). Researchers typically use a held-out portion of the above corpora or specific verses to evaluate quality. Evaluation is often done via **Mean Opinion Score (MOS)** listening tests and comparative spectrogram analysis. For instance, Debnath et al. report MOS ratings from human evaluators to judge their TTS output ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=training%20and%20inference%3B%203,5%20hours%20only)). In lieu of formal benchmarks, one approach is to compare synthesized speech to professionally recorded recitations of well-known texts (like an authentic recitation of a Vedic hymn) and measure prosody similarity. The availability of paired text and recorded chant (for example, the *Samaveda* or *Vedic mantra* audio with text) could serve as an evaluation set for future systems that attempt **chant synthesis**. Additionally, the IIT Bombay corpus can be used to create a test set of varied sentences to objectively evaluate pronunciation accuracy and naturalness. As the field matures, we may see shared tasks or challenges introduced (perhaps under initiatives like the *Bhashini* project) to benchmark Sanskrit TTS on both intelligibility and expressiveness.

**Tools for Annotation and Prosody Labeling:**

- **Phonetic and Prosodic Annotation** – Developing a prosody-aware TTS often requires annotation of speech data with phonetic and prosodic markers. A notable tool is **SIToBI (Segmentation with Intensity, Tones and Break Indices)**, a speech prosody annotation tool created as part of the Bhashini project ([SIToBI - A Speech Prosody Annotation Tool for Indian Languages The current work is carried out as a part of the project titled, “Prosody Modeling”, under the sub-project of the NLTM BHASHINI project, titled, “Speech technologies in Indian languages”, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, with reference number, 11(1)/2022-HCC(TDIL).](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09661v1#:~:text=can%20be%20a%20laborious%20task,a%20result%2C%20other%20languages%20spoken)) ([SIToBI - A Speech Prosody Annotation Tool for Indian Languages The current work is carried out as a part of the project titled, “Prosody Modeling”, under the sub-project of the NLTM BHASHINI project, titled, “Speech technologies in Indian languages”, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, with reference number, 11(1)/2022-HCC(TDIL).](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09661v1#:~:text=proposed%20Segmentation%20with%20Intensity%2C%20Tones,While%20the%20current%20work)). SIToBI extends the familiar ToBI framework to Indian languages, which are mostly syllable-timed. It can automatically generate time-aligned phoneme, syllable, and word boundaries from audio, and label prosodic features like pitch contours (tones) at the syllable level and break indices (pauses) between words ([SIToBI - A Speech Prosody Annotation Tool for Indian Languages The current work is carried out as a part of the project titled, “Prosody Modeling”, under the sub-project of the NLTM BHASHINI project, titled, “Speech technologies in Indian languages”, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, with reference number, 11(1)/2022-HCC(TDIL).](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09661v1#:~:text=proposed%20Segmentation%20with%20Intensity%2C%20Tones,While%20the%20current%20work)). In evaluations on Hindi, Tamil, and Indian English, the tool’s automatic annotations were found to be close to manual ones ([SIToBI - A Speech Prosody Annotation Tool for Indian Languages The current work is carried out as a part of the project titled, “Prosody Modeling”, under the sub-project of the NLTM BHASHINI project, titled, “Speech technologies in Indian languages”, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, with reference number, 11(1)/2022-HCC(TDIL).](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09661v1#:~:text=languages%20are%20syllable,timed%20languages%20as%20well)) ([SIToBI - A Speech Prosody Annotation Tool for Indian Languages The current work is carried out as a part of the project titled, “Prosody Modeling”, under the sub-project of the NLTM BHASHINI project, titled, “Speech technologies in Indian languages”, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, with reference number, 11(1)/2022-HCC(TDIL).](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09661v1#:~:text=in%20India%20may%20also%20exhibit,timed%20languages%20as%20well)). Although Sanskrit was not explicitly included, SIToBI’s methods are language-agnostic and could be applied to Sanskrit recordings (especially given Sanskrit’s phonetic similarity to other Indic languages). Using such a tool, one could label a corpus of Sanskrit poetry recitations with pitch curves and phrase breaks, creating a **prosody-labeled dataset**. This would be extremely useful for training a TTS model to learn where to raise or lower pitch and where to pause in Sanskrit text. Apart from SIToBI, standard tools like **Praat** (for manual phonetic annotation) and **Montreal Forced Aligner** (for aligning audio to text at phone level) are also applicable to Sanskrit, given a pronunciation dictionary. Indeed, the creation of a Sanskrit pronunciation dictionary (mapping Devanagari spellings to phonemes) has been an outcome of earlier TTS work (Mishra’s G2P module) ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=knowledge%20discourse%20of%20Sanskrit,phoneme%20word%20by%20word%2C%20thus)), which can feed into such alignment tools.

- **Metrical Pattern Labeling Tools** – As discussed, **Chandojñānam** and similar tools are effectively “annotators” for poetic meter. They can take raw Sanskrit text and output labels indicating the meter name and the sequence of light (laghu, marked as `L`) and heavy (guru, `G`) syllables. These labels can be thought of as a target prosodic pattern. In a TTS context, one could use Chandojñānam’s output to automatically mark each syllable of a training text as short or long, providing supervision to a duration model in the TTS. For example, if a line is in **śloka** meter (Anuṣṭubh), the tool would label the syllable pattern, and the TTS duration model can be trained to make `L` syllables ~half the length of `G` syllables in synthesis, reproducing the rhythm. Such integration of meter tagging ensures the **rhythmic constraints** of Sanskrit poetry are adhered to. Chandojñānam is available as a Python library (`chanda` on GitHub) ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=available%20for%20use%20at%20https%3A%2F%2Fsanskrit,The)), making it straightforward to incorporate into NLP pipelines. Another related tool is **“Sanskrit Meter Recognizer” by Mishra (2007)**, an early meter tagging program ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=Keshav%20S%20Melnad%2C%20Pawan%20Goyal%2C,Computational)) – though not widely available now, it was one of the first to automate identifying the chandas of a given verse. The continuation of these efforts (Rajagopalan’s tool, Neill’s *Skrutable*) in recent years provides multiple options for researchers to *annotate metrical structure* in Sanskrit corpora.

- **TTS Development Toolkits** – For building the TTS systems themselves, researchers have adapted general frameworks to Sanskrit. The Festvox/Festival suite was used in early work (with custom modules for Sanskrit as noted) ([diwakarji.com - Research](https://www.diwakarji.com/research#:~:text=Studies%2C%20JNU,the%20Festival%20Speech%20Synthesis%20Engine)). Today, open-source deep learning toolkits like **Coqui-TTS** and **ESPnet** support training Tacotron, FastSpeech, etc., which can be applied to Sanskrit given the right data. There are community contributions, such as *avinashvarna’s sanskrit_tts* Python library, which wraps cloud TTS APIs for Sanskrit text ([avinashvarna/sanskrit_tts: Sanskrit text to speech - GitHub](https://github.com/avinashvarna/sanskrit_tts#:~:text=avinashvarna%2Fsanskrit_tts%3A%20Sanskrit%20text%20to%20speech,TTS%20%C2%B7%20Bhashini%20AI%20TTS)) (though not an academic work, it indicates interest in deploying Sanskrit TTS). On the alignment front, the **Indic NLP Library** and **Aksharamukha** tool ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=Anand%20Mishra,com%2Fvirtualvinodh%2Faksharamukha%2C%20jul%2023)) help in converting Sanskrit text among scripts and romanization, which is useful for preparing phonemic transcripts. For prosody evaluation, one can use tools like **DTW (Dynamic Time Warping)** on pitch contours to compare synthesized vs. reference chant melody, or employ the above SIToBI to label both and then compute differences. While these are not “off-the-shelf” benchmarks, they form a toolkit that a researcher can use to quantify how well a TTS is handling prosody.

In summary, the ecosystem for *Swara TTS* development is growing. Academic research has laid the groundwork in Sanskrit TTS and highlighted its challenges ([[2106.05852] Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights](https://ar5iv.org/abs/2106.05852#:~:text=,investigate%20the%20role%20of%20different)) ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=Sanskrit%20is%20a%20heritage%20language,9)). New models are achieving quality synthesis with limited data ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=training%20and%20inference%3B%203,5%20hours%20only)), and there’s increasing focus on *expressive* speech – capturing the rich prosody of Sanskrit’s metrical texts ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/koch22_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=regulated%20form%20with%20parallel%20structures,syn%02thesis%20model%20is%20developed%20within)) ([[1409.4169] An Algorithm Based on Empirical Methods, for Text-to-Tuneful-Speech Synthesis of Sanskrit Verse](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/1409.4169#:~:text=language%20itself,contents%20of%20Sanskrit%20verses%20otherwise)). Computational prosody modeling, whether through rule-based templates or learned embeddings, provides techniques to incorporate **Chandas** (meter) and **Swara** (intonation) into TTS ([[2204.01271] Into-TTS : Intonation Template Based Prosody Control System](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01271#:~:text=,the%20text%20encoder%20output%2C%20synthesizes)) ([](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/korotkova24_interspeech.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20We%20introduce%20an%20unsupervised,the%20examples%20on%20our%20demo)). Crucially, the availability of open datasets ([Vāksañcayaḥ ~ Sanskrit Speech Corpus by IIT Bombay](https://www.cse.iitb.ac.in/~asr/#:~:text=V%C4%81ksa%C3%B1caya%E1%B8%A5%20,radio%20program%2C%20extempore%20discourse%2C%20etc)) ([[2212.03558] Low-Resource End-to-end Sanskrit TTS using Tacotron2, WaveGlow and Transfer Learning](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/2212.03558#:~:text=The%20dataset%20used%20is%20the,given%20in%20Table%20%2024)) and tools for annotation – from meter tagging ([](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14924#:~:text=1%20Introduction%20Majority%20of%20the,the%20correctness%20to%20some%20extent)) to prosodic labeling ([SIToBI - A Speech Prosody Annotation Tool for Indian Languages The current work is carried out as a part of the project titled, “Prosody Modeling”, under the sub-project of the NLTM BHASHINI project, titled, “Speech technologies in Indian languages”, funded by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, Government of India, with reference number, 11(1)/2022-HCC(TDIL).](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09661v1#:~:text=proposed%20Segmentation%20with%20Intensity%2C%20Tones,While%20the%20current%20work)) – means that researchers can train and evaluate systems that not only pronounce Sanskrit correctly but also *speak it with the intended rhythm and melody*. These resources and studies from Indic NLP, speech synthesis, and digital humanities together support the development of **Swara TTS** – a system that would bring Sanskrit’s sonic beauty to life through technology.
